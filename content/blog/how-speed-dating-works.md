Speed dating is a simple idea that people are often nervous about because they do not know the mechanics. This is the mechanics.

One caution throughout: **formats vary enormously between organisers.** Nothing below is universal. Everything below is worth confirming with whoever is running the event you are considering.

## The basic format

You arrive at a venue with a group of people. Over the course of the evening you have a series of short one-to-one conversations, each with a different person. When time is up, a signal sounds and one side of the room moves on.

Common variables:

- **Conversation length:** typically three to eight minutes.
- **Number of conversations:** typically six to twenty.
- **Total length:** usually 90 minutes to three hours, including arrival and a break.
- **Group size:** from a dozen people to well over a hundred.

Shorter conversations mean more of them and a faster read. Longer ones mean fewer people and more depth. Neither is better; they suit different temperaments.

## What you do during the conversations

Most organisers give you a card, a form or an app to note, privately, whether you would be happy to be introduced to each person. Nobody sees your answers but the organiser.

The conversation itself is just a conversation. You are not being scored, and there is no expectation of covering set ground.

## How matching usually works

This is the part first-timers most often misunderstand.

Typically: after the event, you submit your selections. Where two people have each indicated interest in the other, the organiser shares contact details with both. Where only one has, nothing happens and the other person is not told.

**That last point is the whole design.** Nobody is informed that they were not selected, which is why the format produces far less direct rejection than it appears to. You will not know who chose you and did not match, and they will not know about you.

Timing varies from same-night to several days later.

## Online versus in person

Both exist and they are meaningfully different.

**In person:** you get tone, body language, how someone treats staff, and whether being in a room with them is enjoyable. You also get travel, a venue, and being visible.

**Online:** rounds run in video breakout rooms with the same rotation. More accessible if you are far away, have limited mobility, or would rather not be seen in public early on. But video flattens a lot of what a first meeting is good for, and technical friction eats into short rounds.

If safety or discretion is a concern, online is a reasonable first step. If you are deciding whether you want to spend more time with someone, in person tells you more.

## Registration, preferences and consent

**Registration** ranges from buying a ticket to submitting an application that is reviewed. Curated events screen applicants and do not accept everyone; open events are first-come.

**Preferences** — usually age range and who you are interested in meeting — are typically collected at registration and used to build the room.

**Consent and conduct** are where organisers differ most, and where you should ask directly:

- Contact details are shared **only on a mutual match**. One-sided interest should never result in your details being passed on.
- There should be a stated code of conduct and a way to report someone, during and after.
- There should be a named person present whose job is to handle problems.
- You should be able to skip a round, leave early, or decline a conversation without having to explain.

An organiser who cannot answer these clearly is telling you something useful.

## Practical preparation

A short checklist. **Editorial guidance, not a formula.**

- **Confirm the format** — round length, number of rounds, total time, venue, what happens if you arrive late.
- **Eat beforehand.** Many events do not involve a proper meal, and three hours of talking on an empty stomach is a poor plan.
- **Plan your journey and your exit.** Know how you are getting home. Tell someone where you are going.
- **Bring something to write with**, unless the organiser has confirmed an app.
- **Dress for the venue**, not for a performance. Most people over-formalise.
- **Decide what you actually want** before you go. "Meeting three people I would happily have coffee with" is a better measure of a good evening than "finding someone".
- **Take thirty seconds between rounds** to note one concrete detail about the person you just met. After nine conversations they blur, and specifics are what let you choose well afterwards.
- **Do not try to cover everything.** Three minutes is for deciding whether you want thirty more, nothing else.

## Openers that are not small talk

The failure mode of a short conversation is spending it on job, location and commute. Those are recoverable facts. Use the time on something that reveals a preference.

- "What have you been enjoying lately that you'd recommend?"
- "What does a good Saturday look like for you?"
- "What's something you could talk about for an hour?"
- "What made you come tonight?" — often the most useful, because the answers are candid and vary enormously.
- "What's the best thing you've eaten this week?" — light, specific, and surprisingly revealing.

And one to avoid: asking someone to justify why they are single. It lands as an accusation however it is meant.

## Honest expectations

Speed dating does not guarantee a match, and no format can. What it reliably does is compress the early stage: in one evening you find out whether talking to a number of people is enjoyable, which is information that would otherwise take weeks of messaging to gather.

It suits people who do better in person than in text, who find app conversations exhausting, and who would rather know quickly. It suits people who dislike being observed rather less.

A reasonable expectation for a first event: some pleasant conversations, one or two you would happily continue, and a much better sense of whether the format is for you.
