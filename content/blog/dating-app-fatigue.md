The tiredness is real, and it is worth taking seriously without reaching for a diagnosis.

What follows is not therapy, and nothing here is a clinical claim. It is a practical attempt to break "dating apps are exhausting" into its separate causes — because they are genuinely different problems, and the advice that helps one makes the other worse.

## The two fatigues

The single most useful finding about app use is how differently it lands.

In the Pew Research Center's survey of 6,034 U.S. adults, conducted 5–17 July 2022, **54% of women said they had felt overwhelmed by the number of messages they received**, while **64% of men said they felt insecure about the lack of messages.**

Note the scope before you use it: U.S. adults, mid-2022, self-reported, and describing people who had used these platforms. It is not an Indian sample and it does not describe everyone who dates. But it establishes something important — that two people can use the same app and have close to opposite experiences.

**Fatigue A: too much.** A flood of messages, most of them low effort, some of them unpleasant. The work is triage. The exhaustion is from filtering and from managing a steady trickle of hostility.

**Fatigue B: too little.** Sustained effort meeting silence. The work is output with no feedback. The exhaustion is from repetition and from what the silence starts to imply about you.

They feel identical from inside — *I do not want to open this app* — and they need opposite remedies. Telling someone with Fatigue A to send more messages is useless. Telling someone with Fatigue B to be more selective is worse than useless.

**Before anything else, work out which one you have.** Some people have both, on different apps or in different months.

## Other sources of the same feeling

Beyond the two big ones, several distinct frustrations get filed under the same heading:

- **The conversation-to-meeting gap.** Long text exchanges that never become a plan. This is its own specific drain: it consumes real time and produces no information about whether you actually get on.
- **Evaluation strain.** Being continuously assessed, and continuously assessing, is not a natural mode for most people to sustain.
- **Context collapse.** You are judging someone on six photographs and a line of text, which is neither enough to decide with nor enough to dismiss on.
- **Repetition.** The same opening exchange, many times. Past a certain count, novelty stops compensating.
- **Safety overhead.** Verifying, screening, telling a friend where you are going. Necessary, and genuinely tiring — and unevenly distributed, falling more heavily on women and on anyone whose visibility carries risk.

## What to change

### Set a container rather than a limit

"I'll use it less" fails because it has no edges. A container has edges: *Tuesday and Saturday evenings, twenty minutes, on a laptop.* The specific hours matter less than that the app stops being something you open in every idle moment.

The point is not discipline for its own sake. Continuous low-grade browsing is what converts an app into a habit with no natural endpoint.

### Move to a plan earlier

If the exchange is going reasonably, suggest meeting sooner than feels natural. A short, low-commitment, public meeting produces more real information in thirty minutes than two weeks of messaging.

This also directly addresses the conversation-to-meeting gap, which is the single largest source of wasted effort for most people.

### Change the opening question

Most first messages ask something that can be answered in one word. Ask something that requires a sentence and reveals a preference — what someone actually does with a free Saturday, what they are currently arguing with someone about, what they would happily talk about for an hour.

This helps both fatigues. It gives Fatigue A a basis for triage beyond photographs, and gives Fatigue B something to respond to.

### Narrow deliberately, and say so

Vagueness in a profile is usually an attempt to avoid ruling anyone out. It reliably produces more mismatched contact, not less. Saying what you are actually looking for — including that you are looking for something serious, if you are — reduces volume and improves fit at the same time.

### Take a real pause

Deleting the app in frustration at midnight and reinstalling on Thursday is not a pause. A pause is a decided length — two weeks, a month — after which you return deliberately or do not.

The value is not abstinence. It is interrupting a loop in which the app is used to manage the feeling the app produced.

### Use routes that are not apps

Friends who know you. Interests that put you in a room repeatedly. Structured events where meeting is the stated purpose and the awkwardness is shared rather than individual. None of these replace apps; they change the ratio.

## An optional week

This is **an editorial exercise** — a structured way to notice your own patterns. It is not a therapeutic intervention, it has not been tested, and it will not diagnose anything. Skip it freely. Write answers down; the writing is most of the value.

- **Day 1 — Which fatigue?** Too much, or too little? Be specific about the last time you felt it.
- **Day 2 — The actual cost.** How much time went into the apps last week, and what did it produce? Not to shame yourself. To replace a vague sense with a number.
- **Day 3 — The last good conversation.** When was it, and what made it different? Most people find it was specific rather than smooth.
- **Day 4 — What you are avoiding saying.** Is there something true about what you want that is not in your profile because it might narrow the field?
- **Day 5 — The meeting gap.** Of your last ten conversations, how many became a meeting? If it is very low, the problem is the transition, not the matching.
- **Day 6 — Non-app routes.** Name two places you could meet people that are not an app. Are they available to you this month?
- **Day 7 — Decide.** Continue with changes, pause for a fixed period, or stop. Write down which, and why.

## Some things worth not doing

- **Do not diagnose yourself.** Exhaustion from a repetitive, high-rejection activity is an ordinary response to a repetitive, high-rejection activity.
- **Do not treat apps as the villain.** They solved a genuine problem — they made discovery possible outside your existing circle — and for many people they still work.
- **Do not treat the alternative as a cure.** Structured events, introductions and interest-based meeting have their own frictions. A different format changes the shape of the effort; it does not remove it.

That last point applies to us too. Nines at Nine is a different format, not a remedy. If apps are working for you, keep using them.
